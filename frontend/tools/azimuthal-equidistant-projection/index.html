The azimuthal equidistant projection is the map that keeps distance and direction from one chosen point exact: every other place is drawn at its true great-circle distance and on its true bearing, so straight lines out of the centre are great-circle tracks and a range ring means what it says. On a sphere of radius R the point at angular distance c and azimuth θ from the centre lands at x = ρ sin θ east and y = ρ cos θ north, where ρ = R c; written in latitude and longitude with the centre at φ1, λ0 and the point at φ, λ, the angular distance is c = arccos(sin φ1 sin φ + cos φ1 cos φ cos(λ − λ0)) and the easting and northing are x = R (c / sin c) cos φ sin(λ − λ0) and y = R (c / sin c) (cos φ1 sin φ − sin φ1 cos φ cos(λ − λ0)). R is 6371.0088 km, the IUGG mean radius, so the distances here are great-circle distances on a sphere and not geodesics on the WGS 84 ellipsoid, which differ by up to about 0.3 percent. The radial scale is exactly 1 by construction, while the tangential scale c / sin c grows with distance: about 1.0000 below 100 km, 1.07 at 4,000 km, 1.27 at 7,500 km, 1.57 at 10,000 km and 1.98 at 12,000 km, which is why the map is treated as near a globe inside roughly 10,000 km, visibly distorted between 10,000 and 15,000 km and severe beyond that — the band reported here. The antipode is the one place that cannot be drawn: every azimuth is valid there and the whole circle of radius πR = 20,015.1 km is its image, so the page reports the distance with no direction and an unbounded scale instead of inventing an angle, and a point that coincides with the centre reports zero distance and no bearing. With the centre at a pole the equations collapse to ρ = R (90° − φ) and θ = λ. Latitudes are limited to ±90° and longitudes to ±180°, decimal commas are accepted, and the projection is spherical with no ellipsoid, datum shift or false easting; the page does not draw the map or a graticule.
