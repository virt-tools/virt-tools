How the numbers are built

  • A phon is a decibel-like level, not a loudness. By definition the loudness level in phons of a sound is the sound pressure level of a 1 kHz pure tone that listeners judge equally loud, so at 1 kHz the phon figure and the dB SPL figure are the same number.
  • A sone is a loudness, and it doubles when the sound is judged twice as loud. One sone is the loudness of a 1 kHz tone at 40 dB SPL, so 40 phon is 1 sone by definition.
  • Stevens' power law joins the two. Loudness grows as a power of intensity with an exponent of about 0.3, which is why each 10 phon step multiplies loudness by two: sone = 2^((phon − 40) / 10), and phon = 40 + 10 × log₂(sone). The table in the source is exactly this ladder, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64 sone at 40 to 100 phon.
  • The comparison is a ratio of sones. A level is that many times as loud as the reference when its sone figure divided by the reference's sone figure gives that number; in phon terms every 10 phon on top of the reference doubles it, which is why the level difference and the doublings say the same thing in two units.

The power law is a good fit at and above 40 phon. Below that, near the threshold of hearing, equal-loudness contours flatten and the straight formula overstates how quiet a sound is judged, so levels under 40 phon are indicative rather than measured. The figures describe single tones or narrowband sounds; for music, speech or any broadband signal the loudness needs a critical-band model such as the one in ISO 532 rather than a single sone number. Loudness level only equals dB SPL at 1 kHz — at other frequencies the phon figure comes from a contour, not from the sound pressure alone.
