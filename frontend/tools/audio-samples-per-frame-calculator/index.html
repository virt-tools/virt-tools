Samples per frame is the audio sample rate divided by the frame rate. At 48 kHz, 25 fps divides into exactly 1,920 samples, while 29.97 fps gives 1,601.6, so no single whole count fits every frame. The page keeps the arithmetic exact: 23.976 fps is 24000/1001, so 48 kHz at that rate is exactly 2,002 samples per frame, and 44.1 kHz at 29.97 fps is 147,147 samples per 100 frames. Each frame boundary is the frame number multiplied by the samples per frame and rounded down, so frame N starts at sample floor(N x samples per frame), and a frame carries the difference between its own boundary and the next one. The result is a fixed repeating cycle that never accumulates error: 8,008 samples over 5 frames at 29.97 fps makes a pattern of 1,601, 1,602, 1,601, 1,602 and 1,602, and any boundary sits within one sample of the exact position. The page also shows what a flat whole count costs, because most editors and cameras truncate: a fixed 1,601 samples per frame at 48 kHz loses 3 samples every 5-frame cycle, which is 600 samples or 12.5 ms after 1,000 frames. This is arithmetic for aligning double-system sound, building an audio-only timeline and checking a sync offset, not a sample-rate converter: it does not resample, retime, change pitch, apply a 0.1% pull-up or pull-down, model a drop-frame timecode or account for a converter's own clock, and frame numbers are plain integers rather than timecodes. Everything runs in your browser with no storage, uploads or network requests.
