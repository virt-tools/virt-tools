    How the two-ray loss is worked out

    • Two rays, one ground plane. The receiver sees the direct ray and the ray reflected off flat ground. Their path difference grows with the span, so beyond the breakpoint they arrive almost out of phase and the sum falls away twice as fast as free space: loss grows 40 dB per decade of distance instead of 20.
    • The breakpoint. The two models meet at dbp = 4π·ht·hr / λ, with both heights and the wavelength in the same unit. Closer than that the result follows the free-space law 20·log10(4πd/λ); further away it follows the plane-earth law 40·log10(d) − 20·log10(ht) − 20·log10(hr), with the span and heights in metres. Both branches give the same loss at the breakpoint.
    • Power at the receiver. Pr = Pt + Gt + Gr − L, with the path loss quoted between isotropic antennas so the two gains are counted once, here. One foot is exactly 0.3048 metres, and every length can be entered in either unit.
    • What it assumes. Flat, smooth ground, no obstruction, antennas well above the ground and inside the far field, and a reflection that is total. Terrain, buildings, vegetation and the curved earth all change the answer, so treat this as a planning estimate rather than a measured prediction.
