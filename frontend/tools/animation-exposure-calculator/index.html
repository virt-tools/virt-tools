    How exposure timing works

    • The hold. A drawing held for two frames is “on twos”. The sheet advances one drawing every exposure frames, so a 24 fps scene on twos needs 12 drawings for every second of screen time. Threes give 8 drawings a second at the same rate; ones give 24.
    • Frames from a length. The scene length is multiplied by the frame rate and rounded to the nearest whole frame, because a shot can only be an integer number of frames long. The drawing count is the frame count divided by the hold, rounded up: the last drawing covers whatever frames are left.
    • A short last drawing. If the frames do not divide evenly by the hold, the final drawing sits for fewer frames than the rest. The tool names it rather than hiding it, so you can trim a frame, hold the last drawing anyway, or accept the uneven ending.
    • Drawing count to runtime. In the other direction the frames are simply the drawings times the hold, and the runtime is those frames divided by the frame rate — exact, with no rounding of its own.
    • What it does not do. No fractional or drop-frame rates (23.976 and 29.97 are not supported), no camera-move timing, no audio or lip-sync breakdown, and no shooting-schedule arithmetic. Everything runs in your browser with no storage, uploads or network access.
