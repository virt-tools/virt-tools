Frequency weighting is a fixed correction added to a measured level so that a meter reads closer to what the ear hears. A-weighting follows the 40-phon equal-loudness curve: it takes about 19 dB off at 100 Hz and 39 dB at 31.5 Hz while leaving the midrange alone, which is why environmental and workplace noise is usually reported in dB(A). C-weighting is much flatter, within about 3 dB from 31.5 Hz to 8 kHz, and is used for peak levels and loud low-frequency sound. Z-weighting is flat by definition and reports the level as measured. The corrections here come from the analytic weighting functions of IEC 61672-1: R_A(f) = 12194²f⁴ / ((f² + 20.6²) sqrt((f² + 107.7²)(f² + 737.9²)) (f² + 12194²)) with 20 log10 R_A and R_C(f) = 12194²f² / ((f² + 20.6²)(f² + 12194²)) with 20 log10 R_C, each normalised to 0 dB at 1 kHz (the offsets published as +2.00 dB and +0.06 dB); they follow the standard's nominal table to within 0.3 dB from 10 Hz to 20 kHz, so a printed value can differ from a published table in the last tenth of a decibel. A weighted reading is the sum of the weighted band levels, so for a single band the arithmetic is one addition, while a full spectrum is corrected band by band and then combined as energies. A-weighting is a measurement convention rather than a loudness model: it was derived from pure tones at low levels and is used well outside that range, so it says little about how noise is perceived at high levels.
