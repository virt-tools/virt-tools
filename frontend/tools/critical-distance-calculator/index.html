Work out where a room's reverberant field catches up with the direct sound, and what that means for microphone distance in a studio, a hall or a bedroom.

Q is 1 for an omnidirectional source, about 3 for a cardioid microphone and higher for tighter patterns. RT60 is the time for the room to fall 60 dB after the source stops. If you only have the room's dimensions, its RT60 is what the room actually measures, not what a formula promises.

In a diffuse field the direct sound falls 6 dB per doubling of distance while the reverberant sound stays put, so the energy balance depends on one length. Writing A for the room's equivalent absorption area in square metres, Sabine's relation gives A = 0.161 x V / RT60 for a volume V in cubic metres, and the critical distance is dc = 0.25 x square root of (Q x A / pi), which is about 0.141 x square root of (Q x A). At that distance the direct and reverberant sound pressure levels are equal; twice as far the room leads by 6 dB, half as far the source leads by 6 dB. The direct-to-reverberant ratio at a distance d is therefore 20 log10 of dc / d. Because A is derived from RT60 rather than from surfaces and absorption coefficients, the result assumes a diffuse, fairly live room with absorption well below unity: small dead rooms, strong specular reflections, room modes, a directional loudspeaker array or an audience change the real figure, and a cardioid microphone's Q only holds on its axis.
