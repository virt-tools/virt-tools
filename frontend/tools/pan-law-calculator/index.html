Pan Law Calculator

Pan a mono signal across the field and see what the law does to it: each channel's gain, the level change when the mix folds to mono, and the level change heard on two speakers.

0 is centre, -100 is hard left and +100 hard right. The law sets how far each channel drops at the centre, and so whether the level holds on two speakers or in a mono fold-down. Constant power is the usual DAW default; the compromise is the classic console law.

With t = (pan + 100) / 200 running from 0 at hard left to 1 at hard right and a pan angle a = t x 90 degrees, each law sets the two channel gains. The -3 dB constant-power law uses cos(a) and sin(a), so the squared gains sum to 1 at every position and a centred signal sits 3.01 dB down in each channel. The -6 dB linear law uses 1 - t and t, so the gains sum to 1 and a centred signal sits 6.02 dB down in each channel. The -4.5 dB compromise is the geometric mean of the two, the square root of (1 - t) cos(a) on the left and of t sin(a) on the right, which leaves each channel about 4.5 dB down at the centre. All gains are relative to the unpanned signal. The mono fold-down adds the channels as amplitudes, 20 log10(gL + gR), which is what a mono sum of identical, in-phase content does. The two-speaker figure adds their powers, 10 log10(gL^2 + gR^2), which assumes the speakers radiate independently at the listening position. Between the two, constant power holds the two-speaker level flat and folds up to 3.01 dB hot in mono, linear holds the mono sum flat and loses up to 3.01 dB on two speakers, and the compromise splits both errors at about 1.51 dB. Consoles and DAWs differ in detail: some compensate the taper, some do not, and some let the law be chosen per channel. This page models the taper only, does not measure loudness, and assumes one mono source panned between two channels. Everything is calculated in your browser; nothing is sent or stored.
