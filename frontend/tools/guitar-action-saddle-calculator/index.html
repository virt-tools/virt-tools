    How the adjustment is worked out

    • A string is a straight line. Between the nut slot and the saddle, the string sits above the frets on a gentle slope, so its height at any fret is a weighted average of the height at the two ends. Close to the nut the nut slot sets almost all of it; close to the saddle the saddle does.
    • Where a fret sits. In 12-tone equal temperament fret f sits a fraction x = 1 − 2^(−f/12) of the scale length from the nut, so the 12th fret is exactly halfway and the 24th is three quarters of the way to the saddle.
    • From action change to movement. Because the height at the fret changes by x times a saddle movement, moving the saddle by change / x shifts the action at that fret by the amount you asked for. The nut behaves the same way with 1 − x in place of x. At the 12th fret both factors are one half, which is why the well-known rule is twice the change at the twelfth.
    • Scale length cancels out. The multipliers are fractions of the scale, so a 24.75 in and a 25.5 in instrument need the same saddle travel for the same action change; only the fret number matters.
    • Set relief first. A truss-rod adjustment bends the middle of the neck, which this straight-line model does not include. Set relief, let the neck settle, then measure the action. Measure from the top of the fret to the underside of the string, fretting nothing.
    • What it does not do. No saddle position or intonation (fore-aft) maths, no string-gauge or tension effects (see the string tension calculator), no fret wear, no nut-slot angles, and no zero-fret or capo-compensated instruments where the string leaves a fret rather than the nut.
