Speaker Boundary Interference (SBIR) Calculator

Low frequencies radiate from a loudspeaker in every direction, so part of the sound bounces off the nearest wall, floor or ceiling and arrives just after the direct sound. Where that reflection is half a wavelength behind, it cancels the direct sound and scoops a notch out of the response. Enter the distance from the speaker to one boundary and the page gives the first null, the higher notches and peaks, the reflection delay, and how close the speaker must sit for the notch to land above a frequency you choose.

How the null is worked out. The reflection is modelled as an image source behind the boundary, so the reflected path is longer than the direct path by twice the perpendicular distance to that surface. Cancellation happens where that difference is half a wavelength, which puts the first null at f = c / (4d), with c = 343 m/s (20 °C) and d in metres; the higher notches fall at odd multiples of that frequency (3f, 5f) and the reinforcement peaks at even multiples (2f, 4f). A real room measures the actual direct and reflected paths rather than assuming 2d, so a side wall or an off-axis listener shifts the figures, and the depth of each notch depends on how strong the reflection is against the direct sound. Move the speaker closer to the boundary and the notch climbs to a frequency the speaker radiates less energy into and where absorption behind it still works; move it far enough out and the notch drops below the range the speaker reproduces. Because the direct and reflected sounds arrive together, an equaliser boost at the notch lifts the reflection as well, so placement and treatment are the only fixes. The figure assumes a flat, hard boundary and a single reflection: furniture, soffits, a rear port and the second boundary each add their own comb.
