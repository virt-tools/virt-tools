A passive two-way crossover splits the amplifier's output into a low-pass branch for the woofer and a high-pass branch for the tweeter, and a power amplifier drives both from an output impedance close enough to zero to treat as an ideal voltage source. A first-order (6 dB per octave) network needs one series reactance per branch, sized so its reactance equals the driver's impedance at the crossover frequency: L = Z / (2πf) for the woofer's series inductor and C = 1 / (2πfZ) for the tweeter's series capacitor, which puts each branch 3 dB down at the crossover. A second-order (12 dB per octave) Butterworth section adds a shunt element across the driver and scales both reactances by √2: the woofer gets a series inductor of √2Z / (2πf) with a shunt capacitor of 1 / (√2 · 2πfZ), and the tweeter the mirrored pair, a series capacitor of 1 / (√2 · 2πfZ) with a shunt inductor of √2Z / (2πf). With those values each branch is a maximally flat second-order filter: 3 dB down at the crossover, 12 dB per octave above or below it, and no peak. Crossover tables in some books derive the shunt capacitor with the amplifier and the driver both taken as the driver's impedance instead, which makes that capacitor exactly twice the value here — the series elements agree, so if your reference disagrees only on the shunt, that is the reason. The tweeter pad is a constant-impedance L network: with the driver impedance Z and a voltage ratio k = 10−dB/20, the series resistor is Z(1 − k) and the shunt resistor is Zk/(1 − k), so the pad still presents Z to the high-pass branch and the capacitor and inductor values stand unchanged. Inductors and capacitors are treated as ideal, with no winding resistance, ESR, tolerance drift or saturation, and the drivers are taken as pure resistances: a real driver's impedance is far from flat — a nominal 8 Ω woofer can read 30 Ω at its resonance and 10 Ω an octave above it — so measure the drivers at the crossover frequency before ordering parts, and treat these values as a starting point for acoustic measurement, since the acoustic crossover is where the two drivers' own responses meet.
