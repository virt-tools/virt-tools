    How the requirement is worked out

    • Sensitivity is a ratio of level to drive. Quoted per milliwatt, a headphone produces S dB SPL from 1 mW, so a level L needs 10^((L − S)/10) milliwatts. That is a power law: ten decibels louder is exactly ten times the power, twenty decibels is a hundred times.
    • Per volt is the same headphone, measured differently. Quoted per volt rms, the level needs 10^((L − S)/20) volts, because voltage in decibels is 20 log10. The two conventions are tied to the load: S(dB/V) = S(dB/mW) + 10 log10(1000 / R) for an impedance R, so a 32 Ω headphone loses about 15 dB when the spec is rewritten per volt.
    • Voltage, current and power are one fact. For a sine of V volts rms into R ohms, the power is V² / R and the current is V / R. Peak voltage is √2 times the rms figure, which is the swing an amplifier actually has to reproduce inside its supply rails.
    • Why headroom is separate. The level you type is an average; music peaks run well above it. The default 12 dB multiplies the voltage by about 4 and the power by about 16, so an amp chosen for the average alone will clip on transients. Every number on this page includes the headroom you enter.
    • Why the voltage figure decides more than the power figure. Amplifiers are usually specified in volts into a load, and a power rating quoted at 32 Ω can halve or worse into 300 Ω. Compare the voltage this returns with the voltage the amplifier is rated for at your impedance, not with its headline wattage.
    • What it does not do. No damping-factor or output-impedance effects, no frequency-response changes, no distortion or noise floor, no amplifier shopping list and no claim about what is safe to listen to. Everything runs in your browser with no storage, uploads or network access.
