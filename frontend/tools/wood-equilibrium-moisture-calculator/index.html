Wood is hygroscopic. Below the fibre saturation point of about 30% moisture content the cell walls take up or release water until the wood is in equilibrium with the air, and that equilibrium moisture content (EMC) is set far more by relative humidity than by temperature. The USDA Wood Handbook fits the relationship with the Hailwood–Horrobin two-hydrate sorption model, EMC = (1800 / W) [Kh / (1 − Kh) + (K1Kh + 2K1K2K²h²) / (1 + K1Kh + K1K2K²h²)], with h the relative humidity as a fraction and W, K, K1 and K2 polynomials in temperature. Its table 4–2 lists the result from 30 °F to 210 °F and 5% to 95% humidity, and the handbook says the values are close enough to use for any species. The fit is an average of wetting and drying, because a board that has been drying holds a point or two more water at the same humidity than one that has been gaining it (sorption hysteresis). EMC is where a board ends up after days to weeks of steady air — thick stock, finish and still air slow the change — so it is not a meter reading, and the only way to know the moisture in a particular board today is to measure it.
